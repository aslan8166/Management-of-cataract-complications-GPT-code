# Choroidal (Uveal) Metastasis from Male Breast Carcinoma: A Systematic Review of Reported Cases (1952–2025)

> Prepared following the **Academic Research Suite — `deep-research` skill, `systematic-review` mode** (PRISMA 2020). Literature was identified through **PubMed/MEDLINE**, **Scopus**, **Web of Science Core Collection**, and a **supplementary web / grey-literature search** (Google Scholar–style; see §2.2). Because the included evidence consists entirely of single-arm case reports and small case series, quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible; a **structured narrative synthesis (SWiM)** was performed, with **JBI Critical Appraisal for Case Reports** used in place of RoB 2 / ROBINS-I (documented protocol deviation), and **GRADE** applied to rate certainty.

---

## Abstract [PRISMA Item 2]

### Background
Male breast cancer accounts for roughly 0.5–1% of all breast cancers. The uvea (predominantly the choroid) is the most common site of intraocular metastasis in adults, and breast carcinoma is the leading primary in women. Choroidal metastasis from **male** breast carcinoma is exceptionally rare, reported only as isolated cases and small series, and no consolidated synthesis exists.

### Objectives
To systematically identify and synthesize all reported cases of choroidal/uveal metastasis arising from primary male breast carcinoma, and to describe demographics, tumour characteristics, presentation, management, and outcomes (PICOS in §1.2).

### Data Sources
PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection from inception to **7 June 2026**, plus a supplementary web/grey-literature search (last search date 7 June 2026).

### Study Selection
Primary studies (case reports/series) describing histologically confirmed breast carcinoma in a male patient with choroidal or other uveal metastasis. Reviews, female-only series, and reports of metastasis *to* the breast were excluded.

### Data Extraction and Synthesis
Single-reviewer extraction (AI-assisted) into a predefined form; JBI case-report appraisal; narrative synthesis following SWiM; GRADE certainty rating.

### Results
**25 reports describing approximately 31 male patients** met inclusion criteria (1952–2025). Where reported, patients spanned the **4th–8th decades** (range ~35–76 years; most in their 50s–60s), with **invasive/infiltrating ductal carcinoma** and frequently **hormone-receptor–positive** disease. The interval from breast cancer diagnosis to choroidal metastasis ranged from **concurrent presentation to ~10 years**. **Bilateral and/or multifocal involvement appeared disproportionately common** compared with historical female cohorts. Choroidal metastasis nearly always co-occurred with widespread systemic disease (lung, brain, bone, liver). **External beam / intensity-modulated radiotherapy** was the most frequent local treatment; **systemic therapy** produced documented choroidal regression in selected cases — including **two tamoxifen cases**, one **CDK4/6 inhibitor + fulvestrant** case, and **two chemotherapy cases (one complete regression)**. Prognosis was generally poor (reported survival commonly < 1–2 years), though several recent hormone-receptor–positive cases achieved durable ocular regression on systemic therapy.

### Limitations
Evidence is restricted to uncontrolled case-level data with high risk of publication/selection bias and frequent missing data; effect estimates and pooled survival could not be derived. Supplementary grey-literature searching was not exhaustive and additional non-indexed cases likely exist.

### Conclusions
Choroidal metastasis from male breast carcinoma is a rare manifestation of advanced disease that should be considered in any man with a breast cancer history presenting with visual symptoms — and, conversely, can be a sentinel sign prompting work-up for an undiagnosed male breast primary. Hormone-receptor status should guide systemic therapy; radiotherapy remains the local treatment of choice. Certainty of evidence is **very low (GRADE)**.

### Registration
Not prospectively registered (post hoc review). Prospective registration (e.g., PROSPERO) is recommended before any update — see §5.1.

**Keywords**: male breast cancer; choroidal metastasis; uveal metastasis; ocular oncology; intraocular metastasis; systematic review

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Rationale [PRISMA Item 3]

The uveal tract — and within it the choroid — is the most common site of intraocular metastatic disease in adults, owing to its rich vascular supply (Shields et al., 2022; De Potter, 1998). In large referral cohorts, breast carcinoma is the single most common primary source of uveal metastasis overall, accounting for ~34–37% of cases, followed by lung cancer (Shields et al., 2022; Demirci et al., 2003; Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021). However, these cohorts are dominated by women, reflecting the epidemiology of breast cancer.

Male breast cancer is rare (~0.5–1% of all breast cancers) and tends to present at a more advanced stage, with a higher proportion of hormone-receptor–positive, ductal histology. Choroidal metastasis from a **male** breast primary is correspondingly rare: it is documented only through scattered case reports and a few small institutional series, several of which explicitly note being among only a handful ever reported (e.g., Lakhanpal et al., 1982; Silvestris et al., 2003; Reynard & Font, 1983). Clinicians may therefore overlook breast carcinoma in the differential diagnosis of an intraocular metastasis in a man — where lung and gastrointestinal primaries predominate (De Potter, 1998; Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021).

No prior systematic review has consolidated this literature. A structured synthesis is needed to (a) characterize the clinical phenotype in men, (b) compare it with the better-described female phenotype, and (c) summarize management approaches and outcomes to inform clinical suspicion and care.

### 1.2 Objectives [PRISMA Item 4]

**Primary question:** Among males with primary breast carcinoma and choroidal/uveal metastasis, what are the reported demographic, tumour, presentation, treatment, and outcome characteristics?

PICOS framing (adapted for a descriptive case-based review):

- **Population (P):** Male patients with histologically confirmed primary breast carcinoma.
- **Intervention/Exposure (I):** Presence of metastasis to the choroid or other uveal structures (ciliary body, iris), with or without optic disc/orbital involvement.
- **Comparator (C):** Where available, the published female phenotype of breast-cancer choroidal metastasis (indirect, contextual comparison only).
- **Outcome (O):** (1) clinical/demographic features; (2) tumour features (histology, receptors, laterality, multifocality); (3) interval from primary to ocular metastasis; (4) treatments used; (5) ocular and systemic outcomes/survival.
- **Study design (S):** Case reports and case series (no comparative designs exist).

### 2.4 Selection Process [PRISMA Item 8]

Records were screened by title/abstract, then at full-record level against the eligibility table. Screening was performed by a **single AI-assisted reviewer**; the absence of a second independent screener is a limitation (§4.2). Records from Scopus, Web of Science, and the web search were de-duplicated against the PubMed set by DOI and by author/title/year. For grey-literature records that publisher sites would not serve to the retrieval tool, eligibility (male sex + breast primary + choroidal/uveal metastasis) was confirmed from retrievable title/abstract text; sex-indeterminate records were excluded.

### 2.5 Data Collection Process [PRISMA Item 9]

Data were extracted into a predefined form (§2.6) from PubMed metadata, Scopus/Web of Science records, abstracts, and (for grey literature) retrievable article text. Where fields were unavailable they were coded **NR (not reported)** rather than inferred — consistent with the suite's "gray zone = FAIL" integrity rule (no fabrication of patient-level detail).

RoB 2 (RCTs) and ROBINS-I (non-randomized comparative studies) are inapplicable to single-arm case reports. Per accepted practice for case-based reviews, the **Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports (8 items)** was used. Appraisal was single-reviewer (limitation §4.2).

### 2.8 Effect Measures [PRISMA Item 12]

No comparative effect measures (RR/OR/HR/SMD) could be computed — the evidence is descriptive and single-arm. Outcomes are summarized as **frequencies, ranges, and qualitative direction of effect**.

### 2.9 Synthesis Methods [PRISMA Items 13a–13f]

- **13a Eligibility for synthesis:** all included male cases contributed to the narrative synthesis.
- **13b Data preparation:** patient-level fields tabulated (§3.2); patients within multi-case series counted individually where separable.
- **13c Tabulation/visualization:** structured characteristics table (§3.2); JBI traffic-light table (§3.3).
- **13d Synthesis approach:** **Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM)** — grouping by demographics, tumour biology, laterality, treatment modality, and outcome; meta-analysis explicitly not performed (justification §3.5).
- **13e Heterogeneity:** examined narratively (era of publication, receptor status, treatment modality, indexed vs grey literature).
- **13f Sensitivity:** qualitative — findings re-examined excluding pre-1980 reports, excluding overlapping single-institution series, and excluding grey-literature additions.

### 2.10 Reporting Bias Assessment [PRISMA Item 14]

Formal funnel-plot/statistical assessment is not applicable. Reporting bias is addressed narratively: case reports overwhelmingly favour novel, instructive, or treatment-responsive presentations, almost certainly **overstating favourable local responses** and **underrepresenting unremarkable or rapidly fatal cases**.

### 2.11 Certainty Assessment [PRISMA Item 15]

**GRADE** was applied. Evidence from case reports/series begins at **very low certainty** and was not upgraded given uncontrolled design, imprecision, and high risk of bias.

### 3.5 Results of Syntheses [PRISMA Items 20a–20d]

**Why no meta-analysis [20a]:** Included studies are uncontrolled, single-arm, clinically heterogeneous, and report non-comparable outcomes with frequent missing data. Pooling would create spurious precision. SWiM narrative synthesis is the appropriate method.

**Synthesis findings (SWiM) [20b]:**

1. **Rarity & trajectory.** Across ~70 years only ~31 male patients are reported. Reporting continues into 2023–2025, with recent additions concentrated in grey literature.
2. **Demographics.** Age range ~35–76 y, most in the 5th–6th decades. Series suggest men present **younger** and with a **shorter interval** to uveal metastasis than women (Lam 2006: mean 49.7 y, 2.3 y interval vs ~56 y / ~5.4 y).
3. **Tumour biology.** **IDC predominates**; hormone-receptor positivity is common and clinically pivotal (ER+/PR+/HER2− in Fu 2022; tamoxifen-responsive disease in Cohen 2005, Shome 2007, Ansari 2023). One BRCA2 case (Sanayei 2022).
4. **Laterality/multifocality.** Bilateral/multifocal involvement appears **more frequent in men** than the ~38%/~48% reported in women (Lam 2006; also Schulze, Lakhanpal, Tassinari, Palejwala, IJO 2024).
5. **Presentation.** Blurred/decreased vision typical; occasionally the **sentinel sign** of the breast primary; exudative retinal detachment common.
6. **Systemic burden.** Choroidal metastasis almost always coincided with **widespread visceral/CNS disease**.
7. **Management (direction of effect).** **Radiotherapy (EBRT/IMRT)** is the most frequently used and reliably palliative local modality. In **hormone-receptor–positive** disease, **endocrine therapy** (tamoxifen ×2; CDK4/6i + fulvestrant ×1) and **chemotherapy** (×2, including a complete regression) produced documented choroidal regression, sometimes obviating ocular radiotherapy.
8. **Prognosis.** Generally poor (survival commonly < 1–2 years), though several recent HR+ cases achieved durable ocular regression on systemic therapy.

**Heterogeneity exploration [20c]:** Dominant sources are **publication era**, **receptor status**, and **indexed vs grey literature** (grey-lit cases skew recent and treatment-responsive).

**Sensitivity analyses [20d]:**
- *Excluding pre-1980 reports*: conclusions unchanged.
- *Single-institution overlap (Wills Eye)*: qualitative findings persist.
- *Excluding the 3 grey-literature additions*: core conclusions unchanged, but the endocrine/chemo-response signal and the young-age extreme (35 y) weaken — showing grey literature materially enriches the evidence and that **non-indexed sources should not be omitted**.
- *Excluding the 2 Scopus-only additions*: conclusions unchanged.

### 3.6 Reporting Biases [PRISMA Item 21]

Strong **publication and outcome-reporting bias** is expected: case reports favour instructive successes (e.g., regression on systemic therapy) and novel presentations; unremarkable or rapidly fatal cases are under-reported. The grey-literature additions, skewing toward treatment-responsive cases, likely **accentuate** this optimism. Quantitative reporting-bias tests are not applicable.

## 4. Discussion [PRISMA Item 23]

### 4.1 Summary of Evidence

This first consolidated synthesis identifies ~31 male patients across 25 reports (1952–2025). The phenotype is consistent: **men, most often with invasive ductal and frequently hormone-receptor–positive carcinoma**, presenting with visual symptoms amid **advanced, often bilateral/multifocal, systemically disseminated** disease. Relative to the female phenotype (Demirci et al., 2003; Shields et al., 2022), men appear to present **younger, with a shorter primary-to-ocular interval, and a higher tendency to bilateral/multifocal involvement** (Lam et al., 2006) — indirect comparisons based on small numbers.

Two clinically actionable messages emerge. First, **breast carcinoma should be retained in the differential of intraocular metastasis in men**, where lung and GI primaries otherwise dominate; an amelanotic choroidal mass in a man with a breast cancer history is metastasis until proven otherwise, and can be the sentinel sign of an undiagnosed primary. Second, **hormone-receptor status should drive systemic management**: tamoxifen, CDK4/6 inhibition with fulvestrant, and FEC chemotherapy have each produced choroidal regression, occasionally sparing ocular radiotherapy — while radiotherapy (EBRT/IMRT) remains the reliable local option for sight-threatening lesions.

A methodological message also emerges: **grey/non-indexed literature is a material source for this rare topic.** Three of 25 included reports were absent from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science and were found only by open-web searching.

### 4.2 Limitations

**Limitations of the evidence:**
- Exclusively uncontrolled case reports/series → **very low GRADE certainty**.
- Strong publication/selection/outcome-reporting bias, accentuated by grey-literature inclusion.
- Frequent missing data (receptor status, follow-up, survival), especially pre-2000 and in non-English/grey-literature reports.
- Indirect, non-quantitative comparison with female cohorts.

**Limitations of the review process:**
- **Three databases searched (PubMed + Scopus + Web of Science) plus an ad hoc web/grey-literature search.** The grey-literature search was **not systematic or exhaustive** (no structured Google Scholar protocol, no Embase); **additional non-indexed cases very likely exist.**
- Several grey-literature/publisher pages **could not be retrieved in full** by the tooling (HTTP 403), so some fields rely on abstract-level text.
- **Single AI-assisted reviewer** (no independent double-screening) → risk of selection/extraction error.
- **Not prospectively registered.**
- **Possible patient overlap** among single-institution series.
- One recent report (IJO Case Reports 2024) is included with incomplete author metadata (see references).

### 4.3 Implications

**For practice:**
- Dilated fundus examination + ocular imaging (B-scan US, OCT/EDI-OCT, fluorescein angiography) in men with breast cancer and visual symptoms.
- Consider a breast primary in any man with choroidal metastasis of unknown origin; obtain receptor/IHC characterization to guide therapy.
- Radiotherapy (EBRT/IMRT) for sight-threatening lesions; consider systemic endocrine/targeted therapy first in HR-positive disease, with close ophthalmic monitoring.

**For research:**
- A **prospectively registered (PROSPERO)** update with **dual independent reviewers**, **Embase**, a **systematic Google Scholar grey-literature protocol** (e.g., first 200 hits screened), and **full-text individual-patient-data** extraction would substantially strengthen this synthesis.
- Sex-disaggregated reporting in ocular-oncology registries would enable pooled analysis.

**For policy/guidelines:**
- Ocular-metastasis guidance should explicitly address male breast cancer as a rare but recognized, receptor-driven, partly reversible ocular disease.
