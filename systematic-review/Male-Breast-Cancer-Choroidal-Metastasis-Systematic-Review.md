# Choroidal (Uveal) Metastasis from Male Breast Carcinoma: A Systematic Review of Reported Cases (1952–2025)

> Prepared following the **Academic Research Suite — `deep-research` skill, `systematic-review` mode** (PRISMA 2020). Literature was identified through **PubMed/MEDLINE** (NCBI). Because the included evidence consists entirely of single-arm case reports and small case series, quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible; a **structured narrative synthesis (SWiM)** was performed, with **JBI Critical Appraisal for Case Reports** used in place of RoB 2 / ROBINS-I (documented protocol deviation), and **GRADE** applied to rate certainty.

---

## Abstract [PRISMA Item 2]

### Background
Male breast cancer accounts for roughly 0.5–1% of all breast cancers. The uvea (predominantly the choroid) is the most common site of intraocular metastasis in adults, and breast carcinoma is the leading primary in women. Choroidal metastasis from **male** breast carcinoma is exceptionally rare, reported only as isolated cases and small series, and no consolidated synthesis exists.

### Objectives
To systematically identify and synthesize all reported cases of choroidal/uveal metastasis arising from primary male breast carcinoma, and to describe demographics, tumour characteristics, presentation, management, and outcomes (PICOS in §1.2).

### Data Sources
PubMed/MEDLINE from inception to **7 June 2026** (last search date).

### Study Selection
Primary studies (case reports/series) describing histologically confirmed breast carcinoma in a male patient with choroidal or other uveal metastasis. Reviews, female-only series, and reports of metastasis *to* the breast were excluded.

### Data Extraction and Synthesis
Single-reviewer extraction (AI-assisted) into a predefined form; JBI case-report appraisal; narrative synthesis following SWiM; GRADE certainty rating.

### Results
**20 reports describing approximately 26 male patients** met inclusion criteria (1952–2025). Where reported, patients were typically in their **5th–7th decades** (range ~45–62 years), with **invasive/infiltrating ductal carcinoma** and frequently **hormone-receptor–positive** disease. The interval from breast cancer diagnosis to choroidal metastasis ranged from **concurrent presentation to ~10 years**. **Bilateral and/or multifocal involvement appeared disproportionately common** compared with historical female cohorts. Choroidal metastasis nearly always co-occurred with widespread systemic disease (lung, brain, bone, liver). **External beam radiotherapy (EBRT)** was the most frequent local treatment; **systemic endocrine therapy (tamoxifen; CDK4/6 inhibitor + fulvestrant)** and chemotherapy produced documented tumour regression in selected hormone-receptor–positive cases. Prognosis was uniformly poor (reported survival generally < 1–2 years).

### Limitations
Evidence is restricted to uncontrolled case-level data with high risk of publication/selection bias and frequent missing data; effect estimates and pooled survival could not be derived.

### Conclusions
Choroidal metastasis from male breast carcinoma is a rare manifestation of advanced disease that should be considered in any man with a breast cancer history presenting with visual symptoms — and, conversely, can be a sentinel sign prompting work-up for an undiagnosed male breast primary. Hormone-receptor status should guide systemic therapy; EBRT remains the local treatment of choice. Certainty of evidence is **very low (GRADE)**.

### Registration
Not prospectively registered (post hoc review). Prospective registration (e.g., PROSPERO) is recommended before any update — see §5.1.

**Keywords**: male breast cancer; choroidal metastasis; uveal metastasis; ocular oncology; intraocular metastasis; systematic review

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Rationale [PRISMA Item 3]

The uveal tract — and within it the choroid — is the most common site of intraocular metastatic disease in adults, owing to its rich vascular supply (Shields et al., 2022; De Potter, 1998). In large referral cohorts, breast carcinoma is the single most common primary source of uveal metastasis overall, accounting for ~34–37% of cases, followed by lung cancer (Shields et al., 2022; Demirci et al., 2003; Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021). However, these cohorts are dominated by women, reflecting the epidemiology of breast cancer.

Male breast cancer is rare (~0.5–1% of all breast cancers) and tends to present at a more advanced stage, with a higher proportion of hormone-receptor–positive, ductal histology. Choroidal metastasis from a **male** breast primary is correspondingly rare: it is documented only through scattered case reports and a few small institutional series, several of which explicitly note being among only a handful ever reported (e.g., Lakhanpal et al., 1982; Silvestris et al., 2003; Reynard & Font, 1983). Clinicians may therefore overlook breast carcinoma in the differential diagnosis of an intraocular metastasis in a man — where lung and gastrointestinal primaries predominate (De Potter, 1998; Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021).

No prior systematic review has consolidated this literature. A structured synthesis is needed to (a) characterize the clinical phenotype in men, (b) compare it with the better-described female phenotype, and (c) summarize management approaches and outcomes to inform clinical suspicion and care.

### 1.2 Objectives [PRISMA Item 4]

**Primary question:** Among males with primary breast carcinoma and choroidal/uveal metastasis, what are the reported demographic, tumour, presentation, treatment, and outcome characteristics?

PICOS framing (adapted for a descriptive case-based review):

- **Population (P):** Male patients with histologically confirmed primary breast carcinoma.
- **Intervention/Exposure (I):** Presence of metastasis to the choroid or other uveal structures (ciliary body, iris), with or without optic disc/orbital involvement.
- **Comparator (C):** Where available, the published female phenotype of breast-cancer choroidal metastasis (indirect, contextual comparison only).
- **Outcome (O):** (1) clinical/demographic features; (2) tumour features (histology, receptors, laterality, multifocality); (3) interval from primary to ocular metastasis; (4) treatments used; (5) ocular and systemic outcomes/survival.
- **Study design (S):** Case reports and case series (no comparative designs exist).

### 2.4 Selection Process [PRISMA Item 8]

Records were screened by title/abstract, then at full-record (metadata/abstract) level against the eligibility table. Screening was performed by a **single AI-assisted reviewer**; the absence of a second independent screener is a limitation (§4.2). Disagreements were therefore not adjudicated by consensus; ambiguous records were resolved conservatively in favour of full-record review.

### 2.5 Data Collection Process [PRISMA Item 9]

Data were extracted into a predefined form (§2.6) from PubMed metadata and abstracts. Where abstracts were unavailable (older reports and brief clinical communications), only fields ascertainable from title/MeSH/citation were recorded; all unverifiable fields were coded **NR (not reported)** rather than inferred — consistent with the suite's "gray zone = FAIL" integrity rule (no fabrication of patient-level detail).

### 2.7 Study Risk of Bias Assessment [PRISMA Item 11]

RoB 2 (RCTs) and ROBINS-I (non-randomized comparative studies) are inapplicable to single-arm case reports. Per accepted practice for case-based reviews, the **Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports (8 items)** was used (patient demographics; history/timeline; clinical condition; diagnostic tests/methods; intervention/procedure; post-intervention condition; adverse events; takeaway lessons). Appraisal was single-reviewer (limitation §4.2).

### 2.8 Effect Measures [PRISMA Item 12]

No comparative effect measures (RR/OR/HR/SMD) could be computed — the evidence is descriptive and single-arm. Outcomes are summarized as **frequencies, ranges, and qualitative direction of effect**.

### 2.9 Synthesis Methods [PRISMA Items 13a–13f]

- **13a Eligibility for synthesis:** all included male cases contributed to the narrative synthesis.
- **13b Data preparation:** patient-level fields tabulated (§3.2); patients within multi-case series counted individually where separable.
- **13c Tabulation/visualization:** structured characteristics table (§3.2); JBI traffic-light table (§3.3).
- **13d Synthesis approach:** **Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM)** — grouping by demographics, tumour biology, laterality, treatment modality, and outcome; meta-analysis explicitly not performed (justification §3.5).
- **13e Heterogeneity:** examined narratively (era of publication, receptor status, treatment modality).
- **13f Sensitivity:** qualitative — findings re-examined excluding the oldest pre-1980 reports and excluding overlapping single-institution (Wills Eye) series to check for double-counting.

### 2.10 Reporting Bias Assessment [PRISMA Item 14]

Formal funnel-plot/statistical assessment is not applicable. Reporting bias is addressed narratively: case reports overwhelmingly favour novel, instructive, or treatment-responsive presentations, almost certainly **overstating favourable local responses** and **underrepresenting unremarkable or rapidly fatal cases**.

### 2.11 Certainty Assessment [PRISMA Item 15]

**GRADE** was applied. Evidence from case reports/series begins at **very low certainty** and was not upgraded given uncontrolled design, imprecision, and high risk of bias.

### 3.4 Results of Individual Studies [PRISMA Item 19]

Patient-level results are presented in Table 1 (no quantitative effect estimates are derivable). Key individual signals:
- **Treatment-responsive choroidal metastasis** with *systemic* therapy alone: Paoli (1998, chemotherapy), Shome et al. (2007, tamoxifen → complete scarring), Fu et al. (2022, CDK4/6 inhibitor + fulvestrant).
- **Choroidal metastasis as a marker of disseminated disease:** lung/brain/bone/liver co-involvement in the majority (Kreusel 1999; Oleksy 2010; Palejwala 2013; Sanayei 2022; Fu 2022).
- **Atypical/striking presentations:** 10-year latency with combined orbital + uveal spread (Schlaen & Naves 1986); cortical blindness from occipital metastases coexisting with bilateral choroidal metastases (Palejwala 2013); BRCA2-associated disease (Sanayei 2022).

### 3.5 Results of Syntheses [PRISMA Items 20a–20d]

**Why no meta-analysis [20a/justification]:** Included studies are uncontrolled, single-arm, clinically heterogeneous (era, histology, receptor status, treatment), and report non-comparable outcomes with frequent missing data. Pooling would produce spurious precision. A SWiM narrative synthesis is the appropriate method (PRISMA item 13d).

**Synthesis findings (SWiM) [20b]:**

1. **Rarity and trajectory.** Across ~70 years only ~26 male patients are reported. Early authors described their cases as the 3rd–5th ever reported (Greer 1954; Lakhanpal 1982; Silvestris 2003), underscoring extreme rarity. Reporting has continued steadily into 2022–2025, reflecting persistent clinical novelty rather than rising incidence.

2. **Demographics.** Where age was reported, patients clustered in the **5th–7th decades** (≈45–62 years). Several series note men present at a **younger age** and with a **shorter interval** to uveal metastasis than women (Lam et al., 2006: mean 49.7 y, 2.3 y interval vs ~56 y and ~5.4 y in women).

3. **Tumour biology.** **Invasive/infiltrating ductal carcinoma** predominates; hormone-receptor positivity is common and clinically pivotal (explicit ER+/PR+/HER2− in Fu et al., 2022; tamoxifen-responsive disease in Shome et al., 2007). One BRCA2-associated case is reported (Sanayei et al., 2022).

4. **Laterality/multifocality.** **Bilateral and multifocal involvement appears more frequent in men** than the ~38% bilateral / ~48% multifocal reported in female cohorts (Lam et al., 2006: all 3 bilateral, 4/6 eyes multifocal; also bilateral in Schulze 1956, Lakhanpal 1982, Palejwala 2013). This is a recurring qualitative signal, though sample sizes are tiny.

5. **Presentation.** Blurred/decreased vision is typical; choroidal metastasis was occasionally the **initial or sentinel manifestation** prompting discovery of the breast primary, mirroring the female literature. Exudative retinal detachment is common (Oleksy 2010; O'Brien 2000; Sanayei 2022; Qian & Saab 2025).

6. **Systemic burden.** Choroidal metastasis almost always coincided with **widespread visceral/CNS disease** — a consistent marker of advanced, disseminated male breast cancer.

7. **Management (direction of effect).**
   - **Local:** EBRT is the most frequently used and reliably palliative modality for sight-threatening lesions (Kreusel 1999; Hood 2011; Oleksy 2010; Silvestris 2003). Brachytherapy, PDT, TTT, and enucleation (for blind painful eyes — Scilletta 1989; Reynard & Font 1983) are used selectively.
   - **Systemic:** In hormone-receptor–positive disease, **endocrine therapy** (tamoxifen) and **CDK4/6 inhibitor + fulvestrant** achieved documented choroidal regression, sometimes obviating ocular radiotherapy. Chemotherapy also produced regression in selected cases.

8. **Prognosis.** Uniformly poor; reported survival generally **< 1–2 years**, consistent with female breast-cancer uveal-metastasis cohorts (1-yr ~65–73%, 5-yr ~20–24%) but skewed worse here by the advanced systemic stage at ocular diagnosis in men.

**Heterogeneity exploration [20c]:** The dominant sources of heterogeneity are **publication era** (diagnostic/therapeutic capability) and **receptor status** (determining systemic options). Treatment-response signals concentrate in modern, receptor-characterized cases.

**Sensitivity analyses [20d]:**
- *Excluding pre-1980 reports* (Pillat, Greer, Schulze): conclusions unchanged; these add historical context but minimal extractable data.
- *Accounting for single-institution overlap* (Wills Eye group): even conservatively collapsing potentially overlapping Wills Eye male cases, the qualitative findings (ductal histology, bilateral/multifocal tendency, RT efficacy) persist.

### 3.6 Reporting Biases [PRISMA Item 21]

Strong **publication and outcome-reporting bias** is expected: case reports favour instructive successes (e.g., dramatic regression on systemic therapy) and novel presentations. Unremarkable or immediately fatal cases are under-reported. Quantitative reporting-bias tests are not applicable.

## 4. Discussion [PRISMA Item 23]

### 4.1 Summary of Evidence

This first consolidated synthesis identifies ~26 male patients across 20 reports (1952–2025) with choroidal/uveal metastasis from breast carcinoma. The phenotype is consistent: **older men with invasive ductal, frequently hormone-receptor–positive carcinoma**, presenting with visual symptoms in the setting of **advanced, often bilateral/multifocal and systemically disseminated** disease. Compared with the well-characterized female phenotype (Demirci et al., 2003; Shields et al., 2022), men appear to present **younger, with a shorter primary-to-ocular interval, and with a higher tendency to bilateral/multifocal involvement** (Lam et al., 2006) — though these comparisons are indirect and based on very small numbers.

Two clinically actionable messages emerge. First, **breast carcinoma should be retained in the differential of intraocular metastasis in men**, where lung and GI primaries otherwise dominate; an amelanotic choroidal mass in a man with a breast cancer history is metastasis until proven otherwise, and may even be the sentinel sign of an undiagnosed primary. Second, **hormone-receptor status should drive systemic management**: tamoxifen and, more recently, CDK4/6 inhibition with fulvestrant have produced choroidal regression, occasionally sparing ocular radiotherapy — while EBRT remains the reliable local option for sight-threatening lesions.

### 4.2 Limitations

**Limitations of the evidence:**
- Exclusively uncontrolled case reports/series → **very low GRADE certainty**.
- Strong publication/selection/outcome-reporting bias.
- Frequent missing data (receptor status, follow-up, survival), especially pre-2000.
- Indirect, non-quantitative comparison with female cohorts.

**Limitations of the review process:**
- **Single database (PubMed only)** plus reference screening — Embase/Scopus/Web of Science/Cochrane were not searched (tool constraint). Some cases (e.g., non-indexed or non-English) were likely missed.
- **Single AI-assisted reviewer** for screening, extraction, and appraisal (no independent double-screening) → risk of selection and extraction error.
- **Not prospectively registered.**
- **Possible patient overlap** among single-institution series not fully resolvable from abstracts.
- Extraction relied on abstracts/metadata; full texts were not obtained for every report.

### 4.3 Implications

**For practice:**
- Perform dilated fundus examination and ocular imaging (B-scan ultrasonography, OCT/EDI-OCT, fluorescein angiography) in men with breast cancer and visual symptoms.
- Consider breast primary in any man with choroidal metastasis of unknown origin; obtain receptor/IHC characterization to guide therapy.
- Use EBRT for sight-threatening lesions; consider systemic endocrine/targeted therapy first in HR-positive disease, with close ophthalmic monitoring.

**For research:**
- A prospectively registered, **multi-database** systematic review with **dual independent reviewers** and **individual-patient-data** extraction (including full texts) would substantially strengthen this synthesis.
- International ocular-oncology registries should report sex-disaggregated male breast-cancer uveal-metastasis data to enable pooled analysis.

**For policy/guidelines:**
- Ocular metastasis guidance should explicitly address male breast cancer as a rare but recognized primary, given its receptor-driven, partly reversible ocular disease.
